# The Masters — what the great non-fiction writers actually do

This is a distillation of **techniques**, in our own words, from writers and teachers whose work holds readers. It's not a collection of their sentences: never imitate a living writer's voice or copy their lines. Read the originals; they're listed at the end.

Each entry: **the move**, **why it works**, and **how to use it here**.

---

## Structure and story

### John McPhee: structure before sentences
- **The move:** decide the shape of the piece before writing any of it. Lay out all the material, group it, and find a structure (chronological, thematic, circular, or a "spiral" that returns to a core scene) that the material itself suggests. The opening points at the whole.
- **Why:** readers feel structure as momentum even when they can't see it.
- **Use:** that's why stage 4 (outline) is mandatory. For long pieces, sketch the structure as a diagram. A strong default is to start at a dramatic midpoint, go back to the beginning, then catch up and pass it.

### Ira Glass: anecdote, then reflection
- **The move:** a radio story alternates two building blocks. The **anecdote** (a sequence of actions: "this happened, then this") pulls by sheer momentum because the listener wants to know what's next. The **moment of reflection** says why it matters. Without reflection, the anecdote is trivia; without anecdote, reflection is a lecture.
- **Use:** every podcast act and blog section contains both, in that order. Raise questions inside the anecdote, and answer some of them along the way.

### Malcolm Gladwell: the puzzle frame
- **The move:** open with a puzzle (something that doesn't make sense), propose the obvious explanation, show it fails, then reveal an unexpected one drawn from a surprising field. Alternate story and idea.
- **Why:** a puzzle is an open loop with intellectual stakes.
- **Use:** the Act 2 "turn" in a podcast is often a puzzle resolution. "Why do people with *better* CVs get fewer callbacks?"

### Michael Lewis: idea through character
- **The move:** find the person who embodies the idea (the outsider who saw what everyone missed) and tell the idea through their story, with their quirks and stakes.
- **Use:** pick one story-bank person per piece as the protagonist. One person, followed closely, beats five examples skimmed.

### Erik Larson / Laura Hillenbrand: narrative non-fiction pacing
- **The move:** true events told with novelistic suspense: interleaved timelines, chapter endings that cut away at the moment of tension, foreshadowing ("He would not see her again").
- **Use:** the micro-cliffhanger (see `bridges-and-loops.md`); foreshadowing as an open loop: "That decision would cost her six months."

### Tom Wolfe: the four devices of narrative journalism
1. **Scene by scene:** show it happening rather than summarising history.
2. **Real dialogue:** people revealed by how they talk.
3. **Point of view:** put the reader inside one person's head for a scene.
4. **Status details:** the small objects, gestures and habits that show who someone is (the spreadsheet, the interview shirt ironed twice).
- **Use:** in story beats, check all four. Status details come from transcripts: listen for them.

### Jack Hart (*Storycraft*): the narrative arc
Exposition → rising action (complications) → crisis → climax → falling action → resolution. Every true story with a protagonist, a complication and a resolution can carry this arc, even in 300 words.
- **Use:** story-bank moments are tagged with belief + turning point exactly so they can fill this arc.

### Sarah Koenig (*Serial*) and narrative podcasting: the reporter as character
- **The move:** the narrator's own uncertainty and changing mind becomes a thread. "I wasn't sure. Here's what made me doubt it."
- **Use:** the confession hook, and honest doubt mid-piece, build trust far faster than certainty.

## Clarity and style

### William Zinsser (*On Writing Well*): fight clutter
- **The move:** strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be short, every passive that could be active weakens the sentence. Write as yourself, to a person.
- **Use:** the polish stage's 15–20% cut. Our hedge and clutter checks in the linter.

### George Orwell ("Politics and the English Language")
- **The moves:** never use a metaphor you're used to seeing in print; never use a long word where a short one will do; cut every word you can; prefer active; prefer everyday words to jargon; break any rule rather than write something ugly.
- **Use:** `word-craft.md` and the banned-phrase list are this, enforced.

### Steven Pinker (*The Sense of Style*): classic style and the curse of knowledge
- **The move:** "classic style": the writer shows the reader something in the world, as if pointing at it, conversing as equals. The enemy is the **curse of knowledge**: forgetting what it's like not to know what you know, so you skip steps and use insider terms.
- **Use:** the Sceptical Reader critic. Every term your audience wouldn't use gets explained or replaced. Show the thing, don't talk about talking about it ("In this section we will discuss...").

### Roy Peter Clark (*Writing Tools*): the ladder of abstraction
- **The move:** good writing moves up and down a ladder: concrete at the bottom (a woman, a spreadsheet, 212 rows), abstract at the top (persistence, the job market, readiness). Writing stuck at the top is vague; stuck at the bottom has no meaning. Move between them on purpose. Also: put the strongest words at the end of the sentence; vary sentence length; use subject-verb at the start of sentences.
- **Use:** each section touches both rungs: the detail, then what it means. The "end on the strongest word" rule in polish.

### Chip and Dan Heath (*Made to Stick*): SUCCESs
Ideas stick when they're **S**imple (a core), **U**nexpected (break a pattern), **C**oncrete, **C**redible, **E**motional (about a person, not a statistic), and told as **S**tories.
- **Use:** run the spine sentence and the quote-test line through all six.

### Morgan Housel: story as the delivery system for an idea
- **The move:** open with a short, vivid, often historical story; then extract a single, plainly stated principle; keep sentences short and claims modest; use simple words for hard ideas.
- **Use:** the template for a point-of-view blog: story → principle → why it's true → what to do.

### Paul Graham: write like you talk, and write to think
- **The move:** essays as plain conversation; if you wouldn't say it out loud to a friend, don't write it. Writing is also how you find out what you think: the draft of your reasoning comes before the draft of your prose.
- **Use:** read-aloud test for blogs too. Before the outline, free-write the argument for ten minutes and note where your view changed; that turn is often the angle.

### Richard Feynman: understanding vs knowing the words
- **The move:** to test whether you understand something, explain it simply to someone who knows nothing about it; where you stumble or reach for jargon, that's the gap. Go back to the material and fill it.
- **Use:** the twelve-year-old test in `principles.md`, run on the spine sentence before outlining.

## Persuasion (from the great copywriters)

### Joseph Sugarman: the slippery slide
- **The move:** the only job of the first sentence is to get the second sentence read, and so on. Short first sentences, curiosity at every paragraph end.
- **Use:** the second-line test.

### Eugene Schwartz (*Breakthrough Advertising*): levels of awareness
The audience is either unaware of the problem, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most aware. The opening must meet them where they are: never lead with the product to someone who doesn't yet know they have the problem.
- **Use:** the brief names the audience's awareness level; the hook and the brand's position in the piece follow from it.

### David Ogilvy: specifics sell, research first
Specific facts persuade more than adjectives. Ogilvy's advertising began with deep research into the product and the customer.
- **Use:** stage 2 (mine) comes before any writing.
