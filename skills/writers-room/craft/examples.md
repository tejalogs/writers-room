# Examples — what good looks like

Models learn style from examples far better than from rules: in a 400-author style-imitation study, two examples did most of the work and more added little. So this file shows. It doesn't lecture.

**These examples are written for teaching. Their people and numbers are illustrative, not real.** In real pieces, every detail comes from the story bank or a cited source. Imitate the *moves*, never the content. A brand pack should add 2–3 of its own best approved pieces under "Voice samples" in `BRAND.md`; those outrank this file for voice.

## 1. Before → after pairs

### Hook
**Before:** In today's competitive job market, many candidates find it difficult to secure interviews despite submitting numerous applications.
**After:** She sent 212 applications in four months. Two people wrote back. Both were automated.
**The move:** a specific number, a time span, and a final turn that makes it worse.

### Opening of a blog
**Before:** Networking is an important part of career development. In this article, we will explore why networking matters and share some tips to help you build meaningful professional connections.
**After:** The best job he ever got came from a man he'd met once, at a bus stop, in the rain. They talked for six minutes. That's the uncomfortable truth about hiring: most good jobs move through people before they reach a job board, and nobody tells you which people.
**The move:** a scene first; the claim second, stated plainly; an open loop at the end ("which people").

### Generic paragraph → specific
**Before:** Rejection can be very challenging and can significantly impact your confidence. It's important to stay positive and remember that every rejection is a learning opportunity that brings you closer to success.
**After:** After the ninth rejection she stopped opening the emails. She'd see the subject line ("Your application to...") and archive it unread. By the time anyone gave her real feedback, she'd stopped believing feedback existed.
**The move:** behaviour instead of feelings. One concrete ritual (archiving unread) does the work of three abstract adjectives.

### Bridge between sections
**Before:** Now that we've covered why CVs fail, let's look at how to fix them.
**After:** So the CV wasn't the problem. Which raises a worse question: if it wasn't the CV, what were recruiters actually rejecting?
**The move:** "so" + "which raises" (consequence, then question). The reader is pulled forward by a question, not told where they're going.

### Section ending
**Before:** In summary, role clarity is essential for a successful job search.
**After:** Role clarity fixed his CV in an afternoon. It took three more weeks, and one very awkward phone call, to fix his interviews.
**The move:** close one door, open another. The "awkward phone call" is a loop the next section must pay off.

### Writing for the ear
**Before (written for the eye):** According to the 2025 survey (n=1,240), approximately 63.4% of respondents, primarily recent graduates in non-technical fields, reported receiving no feedback whatsoever following unsuccessful interviews.
**After (written for the ear):** Here's a number that stopped me. Nearly two in three people we surveyed got no feedback after a failed interview. None. Not a line. And most of them were recent graduates.
**The move:** round the number, give it its own sentence, repeat the key idea ("None. Not a line."), move qualifiers to after the point. (The source and sample size go in the show notes.)

### Blog answer block (AEO)
**Before:** There are many reasons why candidates might not hear back after applying, and it often depends on a variety of factors including the industry, the role, and the specific employer.
**After:** **Why don't employers reply to applications?** Most applications are never read by a person: they're filtered out by keyword matching or never reach the top of a pile of hundreds. The fastest fix is to apply to fewer roles, each with a CV rewritten around that job's own words.
**The move:** the question restated; a direct, quotable answer in two sentences; an action. An answer engine can lift it whole, and a human gets a reason to keep reading.

### Close (kicker)
**Before:** Remember, with the right mindset and preparation, you can achieve your career goals. Good luck!
**After:** Last month she sent four applications. Four, not two hundred. She still has the spreadsheet, though. Column F has a new heading now. It says "interview."
**The move:** callback to the opening image (the spreadsheet), the same detail now meaning the opposite.

## 2. Model passages

### Blog: opening of a point-of-view post
> The advice was always the same: it's a numbers game. Apply widely. Something will stick.
>
> So Daniel applied widely. He applied to marketing roles and sales roles, to analyst jobs he half understood, to a position at a company whose name he had to look up afterwards. In eleven weeks he applied to 340 jobs. He got three interviews and no offers, and he came out of it with a conviction that something was wrong with him.
>
> Nothing was wrong with him. Something was wrong with the advice. **A job search isn't a lottery, where more tickets mean better odds; it's closer to a courtship, where trying to impress everyone guarantees you impress no one.**

Why it works: it names the enemy (the advice) in line one; the middle paragraph accelerates with a list of escalating absurdity; the turn arrives in three short words ("Nothing was wrong"); the bold line is the quote-test sentence.

### Blog: a section that answers first
> ## How many jobs should you apply to each week?
>
> Fewer than you think: for most people, five to ten well-matched roles beat fifty loose ones. The reason is time. A tailored application takes about ninety minutes to do properly: research, a rewritten summary, a cover note that mentions something real about the company. Fifty of those is a full-time job on top of the job hunt. So people stop tailoring, and untailored applications are the ones that disappear.

Why it works: the answer comes in the first nine words; the explanation is a chain of "so" consequences; the section ends on a specific, slightly ominous image ("disappear").

### Podcast: cold open
> HOST: The email arrived at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning. She read it on the bus.
> [PAUSE]
> HOST: Four lines. "Thank you for your interest. After careful consideration. Other candidates. Best of luck."
> HOST: It was her fortieth. And this time, she did something none of the advice had told her to do.
> HOST: [LOOP: what-she-did] She replied.
> [MUSIC: in]

Why it works: time and place in one stroke; the rejection compressed to its clichés (we all know them); the stakes ("fortieth"); a loop opened on a two-word line.

### Podcast: the turn in Act 2
> HOST: So here's what she wrote back. Two sentences. "I understand. Could you tell me one thing I could have done better?"
> [CLOSE: what-she-did]
> HOST: Most people never send that email. And I get it. It feels like begging.
> HOST: But think about it from the other side. The recruiter has just turned down forty people. Thirty-nine of them went quiet. One asked a real question.
> [PAUSE]
> HOST: Who do you think they remember?
> [REHOOK]

Why it works: the loop is paid off; the listener's objection is voiced and respected ("I get it"); the perspective flips to the other side; it ends on a direct question to the listener.
