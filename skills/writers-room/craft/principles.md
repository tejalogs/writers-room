# The Principles — the bar every piece must clear

Ranked. When two collide, the higher one wins.

## 1. True beats impressive
Never invent stories, quotes, numbers, people or outcomes. Use the brand's story bank and cited sources. If you don't have it, mark `[NEEDS STORY]` / `[NEEDS SOURCE]`. Readers forgive a missing detail; they never forgive a fake one. AI answer engines also discount claims they can't verify.

**Never invent research the brand supposedly did.** No "our team ran…", no named experiments ("the 7-Day X Autopsy"), no surveys, benchmarks or datasets unless the brand pack or claims table describes the real one, with its method. A made-up study is the most damaging fake of all: it looks like proof, so every number hanging off it becomes a fake too.

**Specific doesn't mean invented.** When the material is thin, be specific about the *idea* (a sharp claim, a named mistake, a precise distinction) rather than inventing specific *facts*. A number with no source is worse than no number.

## 2. Specific beats general
General writing is invisible. Every claim earns a concrete detail: a number, a time, a place, a job title, a verbatim line someone said.

- General: "Many job seekers struggle with rejection."
- Specific: "Her spreadsheet had 212 rows. Column F said 'no reply' 198 times."

Rule of thumb: at least one concrete detail per 100 words.

The same goes for claims about a product: **show, don't declare.**
- Declared: "Our platform provides excellent career guidance."
- Shown: "See which roles match what you can already do, where your skills fall short, and what to practise first."

(Illustrative, for an imaginary careers product. Describe only features your brand pack or the user has confirmed.)

## 3. One piece, one idea
Before drafting, write the **spine sentence**: the single idea the piece exists to land, in 20 words or fewer. Cut everything that doesn't serve it, however good. A piece that says three things says nothing.

## 4. Tension drives everything
Every piece lives in the gap between **what the reader believes** and **what is actually true**. Name both. If you can't state the belief you're overturning, you have a topic, not a piece.

## 5. Every sentence has a job
Each sentence must raise a question, answer one, add a concrete detail, or make the reader feel something. Otherwise delete it, especially sentences that summarise what you just said.

## 6. The audience is the hero; the brand is the guide
The reader's struggle is the story. The brand appears as the guide with a method: late and lightly, never in the hook, rarely more than twice.

Start every piece from **human behaviour** ("why do people keep doing X?"), never from the product's feature list. When the brand does appear, sell the **outcome** (what changes for the reader), not the features. See the persuasion arc in `narrative.md`.

## 7. Write for how it will be consumed
- **Podcast:** for the ear. Short sentences, deliberate repetition, signposting. No formatting does the work.
- **Blog:** for the scanner first, the reader second, the answer engine third. Headings that answer questions; the key sentence of each section in bold.

## 8. Change their mind, then hand them a tool
The reader leaves believing something new and holding one thing to do today. Insight without action is entertainment; action without insight is a listicle.

## 9. Fix the thinking before the words
If the central idea is weak, polishing the sentences won't rescue it. Simple language can carry sophisticated thinking; complicated language often hides shallow thinking.

## 10. Draft hot, edit cold
Draft fast. Edit as a stranger who doesn't care. Cut 20% on the first edit pass, every time.

## The seven tests (every piece, before it ships)

0. **Twelve-year-old test (run first, on the spine):** explain the spine sentence to a bright 12-year-old. If you can't without losing the meaning, you don't understand it well enough yet (Feynman). Go back to the angle.

1. **Competitor swap:** could a rival publish this unchanged under its own logo? If yes, it's generic. Rewrite until it couldn't.
2. **Second line:** does the first line make the reader *need* the second?
3. **Quote:** is there at least one line someone would screenshot or repeat to a friend?
4. **Stranger:** would a busy stranger forward this to someone they care about?
5. **Turn:** can you name the belief at the start and the belief at the end?
6. **Monday:** does the reader know exactly what to do on Monday morning?

## What we refuse to publish

- Listicles of obvious advice with no new insight.
- Motivation without method. Fear without a way out.
- Anything that reads as machine-written: see `craft/banned-phrases.txt`.
