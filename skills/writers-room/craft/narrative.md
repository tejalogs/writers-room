# Narrative Craft — telling true stories so they land

Non-fiction that "magics" the reader almost always does so with story. This file covers how to turn a real moment from the story bank into a scene that works.

## 1. Scene vs summary

- **Summary** compresses time: "Over the next six months she applied for jobs without success."
- **Scene** happens in real time, in a specific place, with action and often dialogue: "Sunday, 11:40 pm. Laptop on the ironing board because the desk was covered in printouts. She hit submit."

Rule: **scene for the moments that matter, summary to get between them.** A piece with no scenes reads like a report. A piece of only scenes has no meaning. Most pieces need 2–4 scenes.

## 2. Building a scene (checklist)

1. **Where and when** in one stroke (no weather reports unless they matter).
2. **One status detail** that tells us who this person is (the ironing board).
3. **Action:** something happens; a verb, not a state.
4. **Dialogue or a verbatim line**, if the source has it.
5. **The turn:** something shifts by the end of the scene, even slightly.
6. **Cut out at the peak**, not after things calm down.

## 3. The character

The reader needs a person to follow. Give them:
- **A want** ("an interview, any interview"),
- **an obstacle** (no replies, no feedback),
- **a belief that's wrong** ("it's a numbers game"),
- **a turning point** (what changed their mind),
- **a change** (what's different now, only as far as the source says).

Describe people by what they do and say, not by adjectives. "Determined" is a claim; "she rewrote the CV at 6 am before her shift" is proof.

## 4. Point of view and distance

Pick one: **close** (inside the person's experience: "She didn't understand. She'd done everything right.") or **observer** (narrator reporting: "She told me she'd done everything right."). Close is more immersive; observer is safer for real people because it stays with what they actually said. Default for real stories: observer, with the person's own words carrying the emotion.

## 5. Time and sequence

- **Chronology is the default** because it's how suspense works: the reader wants to know what's next.
- **Start late:** enter the story as near the crisis as possible, then fill in backstory in a sentence or two.
- **In medias res + flashback:** open at the crisis, step back to how it began, return and continue. (The cold-open pattern.)
- **Withhold, don't hide:** you may delay information to create suspense, but never mislead the reader about facts.

## 6. Pacing

- Speed up with summary, short sentences and white space.
- Slow down at the peak: more detail, shorter paragraphs, one-line paragraphs, `[PAUSE]` in audio. Slowing down at the most important moment is how you tell the reader it matters.

## 7. Dialogue and quotes

- Quote only what's sharp, revealing or emotional. Paraphrase information.
- Verbatim from the source. Don't "improve" real people's words; you may trim with ellipses where meaning is unchanged.
- "Said" is invisible; use it. Not "exclaimed", "revealed", "shared".
- Let the quote land: put it at the end of a paragraph, or alone.

## 8. Meaning: the reflection beat

After each story beat, step back and say what it means, in one or two sentences (the anecdote-then-reflection rhythm). Move up the ladder of abstraction from the specific moment to the general truth, then back down to what the reader should do.

Bad: "This shows that persistence alone isn't enough." (Generic.)
Better: "Two hundred and twelve applications didn't fail because she gave up too early. They failed because every one was aimed at a job she'd already outgrown." (Specific meaning, carried by detail.)

## 9. The ending (the kicker)

Strong endings, in order of preference:
1. **Callback:** return to the opening scene or image, now seen differently.
2. **A final line from the person**, if it carries the turn.
3. **The forward image:** a concrete picture of what happens next.
Never end on a summary of the piece or a generic encouragement ("You've got this!").

## 10. The persuasion arc (when the piece has to sell)

Ads, landing sections, social posts with a CTA and the closing third of a blog follow five moves. Each move earns the next; skipping one is what makes content feel like an advert.

1. **Recognition:** show a situation the audience has lived, so they see themselves ("212 rows in a spreadsheet").
2. **Tension:** a contradiction that makes them question what they believed (the Contradiction Hunt's truth).
3. **Revelation:** a new way to understand the problem ("the CV isn't weak, it's aimed at three jobs").
4. **Solution:** the product or method as the logical next step, and only now. Sell the **outcome** (what changes for them), shown concretely, not a list of features.
5. **Action:** one specific, believable next step. Never invented urgency.

Feature-first writing ("we have AI interview prep, career mapping, skill development") skips moves 1–3 and asks the reader to do the persuading themselves.

## 11. Ethics of true stories

- **Never invent inner states.** Thoughts, feelings and motives ("she'd been careful never to choose", "she felt like a failure") appear only if the source records them or the person said them. Report behaviour and words; let the reader infer the feeling.
- **First person only with real experience.** Write "I" or "we" about an experience only if it's in the story bank or the author's bio. Never give the author anecdotes they didn't have.
- **Advice is fine; label it.** A recommendation that isn't data ("start with five a week") is labelled as yours ("our suggestion") so it isn't read as a statistic.
- **Frequency words need evidence.** "Most people", "almost nobody", "everyone" are statistics in disguise. Cite, soften to what you know, or cut.
- Anonymise fully; change nothing that alters meaning.
- No composites presented as one person. If a pattern comes from many people, say so: "Of the 40 people we spoke to who..."
- Outcomes only as reported. If you don't know how it ended, say that: it's often more honest and more powerful.
